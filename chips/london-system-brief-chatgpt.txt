I want to write a windows app with Claude Code that will teach me how to play and get good at the Chess opening known as the London System. I need an initial brief, can you suggest one?

Absolutely. I’d frame it as a **teaching application rather than a chess database**. The key is that the app should teach you the *ideas and patterns* of the London System, then progressively test whether you actually recognise them.

# London System Chess Trainer — Initial Development Brief

## 1. Purpose

Build a Windows desktop application that teaches me how to play the **London System** in chess and, through structured practice, helps me become genuinely competent at playing it.

The application should act as a **personal chess coach**, not simply a move reference tool.

The primary objective is to teach me:

* The standard London System setup.
* The strategic ideas behind the opening.
* Typical plans for White.
* How to respond to Black's different setups.
* Common tactical motifs and traps.
* Typical middlegame positions arising from the London.
* How to recognise when the standard London plan should be changed.
* How to avoid playing the opening mechanically.

The app should progressively move me from **understanding → recognising → practising → playing → reviewing**.

---

## 2. Target User

The application is initially for one player: me.

Assume that I understand the basic rules of chess and can play a complete game, but I am not an advanced tournament player.

The teaching style should therefore be:

* Clear and practical.
* Beginner-friendly without being simplistic.
* Focused on patterns and ideas rather than memorising long variations.
* Interactive rather than text-heavy.
* Encouraging but honest when I make mistakes.

The application should explain **why** a move is good, not merely tell me which move to play.

---

## 3. Core Teaching Philosophy

The application should teach the London System as a **system of ideas**, not a fixed sequence of moves.

For example, the user should learn concepts such as:

* Developing the dark-squared bishop before playing e3.
* Establishing a strong pawn structure.
* Typical knight development.
* Castling safely.
* When to play Nbd2.
* When to play c3.
* When to play Qc2.
* When the e-file, h-file or kingside attack becomes important.
* Typical central breaks such as e4.
* Understanding the role of the c- and d-pawns.
* Recognising Black's plans and adapting accordingly.

The app should repeatedly reinforce the principle:

> Don't memorise the London. Understand the position.

---

## 4. Main User Experience

The application should have several major modes.

### A. Learn

Structured lessons introducing the London System progressively.

Example lesson sequence:

1. What is the London System?
2. The basic setup.
3. Why the setup works.
4. Development priorities.
5. Castling and king safety.
6. Typical middlegame plans.
7. The e4 break.
8. Kingside attacks.
9. Playing against ...d5.
10. Playing against ...Nf6.
11. Playing against ...g6.
12. Playing against ...Bf5.
13. Playing against ...c5.
14. Common mistakes.
15. Typical tactical patterns.

Each lesson should contain a chessboard where moves can be demonstrated interactively.

---

### B. Practice

The app presents a chess position and asks:

**"What would you play here?"**

The user selects a move on the board.

The application should then explain:

* Whether the move is good.
* The best or recommended move.
* Why it is good.
* What strategic idea it supports.
* What mistake the user made, if applicable.

The system should favour explanations such as:

> "This move is playable, but you are missing the opportunity to prepare e4."

rather than simply:

> "Incorrect. The engine prefers Nbd2."

---

### C. Opening Repertoire

Provide an interactive London System repertoire.

Rather than presenting a huge move tree, organise it around **Black's replies**.

For example:

**1.d4 d5**

Then branches such as:

* ...Nf6
* ...Bf5
* ...e6
* ...c5
* ...g6
* ...Nc6
* early ...Qb6

The user should be able to explore the recommended London response to each setup.

The application should distinguish between:

**"Core London moves"**

and

**"Moves you need to know because Black is challenging your setup."**

---

### D. Tactical Trainer

Create London-specific tactical exercises.

Exercises should be drawn from typical London structures and themes, including:

* Kingside attacks.
* Bxh7+ ideas.
* Ne5 tactics.
* Qc2 attacks.
* e4 breakthroughs.
* Pressure on the h-file.
* Pins and discovered attacks.
* Tactical exploitation of weak dark squares.
* Typical mistakes by Black.

The exercises should teach recognition of recurring patterns rather than isolated puzzles.

---

### E. Play the Opening

Allow the user to play against the computer from London System positions.

Modes:

* Play the first 5 moves.
* Play the first 10 moves.
* Play until the middlegame.
* Play a complete game.

The computer should deliberately use a variety of Black setups so that the player learns to adapt.

After the game, provide a **London System review**, rather than just a generic engine analysis.

For example:

> You reached a good London position, but you spent three moves rearranging pieces instead of beginning your kingside expansion.

---

### F. Mistake Review

The application should remember recurring mistakes.

For example:

**Your recurring problems**

* Moving the same piece twice in the opening.
* Delaying castling.
* Playing c3 automatically when it is unnecessary.
* Missing e4 opportunities.
* Attacking before completing development.
* Not recognising Black's queenside counterplay.

The app should then generate targeted exercises to address those weaknesses.

---

## 5. Chessboard

The chessboard should be the centrepiece of the application.

Requirements:

* Standard interactive chessboard.
* Click/tap to move pieces.
* Legal move validation.
* Move history.
* Take-back.
* Position reset.
* Highlight legal moves.
* Optional coordinates.
* Ability to play variations.
* Ability to explain a move immediately.

The user should be able to step forwards and backwards through variations.

---

## 6. Teaching System

The teaching engine should distinguish between different types of knowledge.

### Memorisation

"What is the normal move?"

### Understanding

"Why do we play this move?"

### Recognition

"What type of position is this?"

### Calculation

"What happens if you play this move?"

### Decision-making

"What is the best plan?"

The application should gradually move from memorisation toward independent decision-making.

---

## 7. Difficulty Levels

Provide several levels.

### Beginner

Focus on:

* Setup.
* Development.
* Castling.
* Basic plans.
* Avoiding obvious mistakes.

### Intermediate

Focus on:

* Choosing between competing plans.
* Recognising Black's setup.
* Timing pawn breaks.
* Tactical opportunities.

### Advanced

Focus on:

* Positional nuances.
* Move-order issues.
* Transpositions.
* Engine-informed improvements.
* Playing against strong defensive setups.

---

## 8. Adaptive Learning

The app should remember what I struggle with.

For example, if I repeatedly miss:

**e4 opportunities**

the application should increase the number of exercises involving e4.

Likewise, if I consistently mishandle:

**Black playing ...c5**

the app should give me more positions involving that structure.

The goal is for the application to behave like a **personal tutor that learns the student's weaknesses**.

---

## 9. Chess Engine

Use a strong chess engine such as **Stockfish** for analysis.

However, engine evaluation should not dominate the user experience.

For example, avoid simply saying:

> +0.72

Instead say:

> White has a small advantage because Black's queenside development is incomplete and White can prepare e4.

Engine information should be available for deeper analysis but should not be the primary teaching mechanism.

---

## 10. London System Knowledge Base

The application should contain a structured knowledge base covering:

* Opening principles.
* Standard London positions.
* Typical plans.
* Common Black responses.
* Tactical motifs.
* Typical mistakes.
* Middlegame structures.
* Model games.
* Key positions.

Ideally, positions should be tagged by concepts such as:

`development`

`king_safety`

`e4_break`

`kingside_attack`

`black_c5`

`black_bf5`

`queenside_counterplay`

`piece_redevelopment`

This will allow the teaching engine to select appropriate exercises.

---

## 11. Progress Tracking

Track:

* Lessons completed.
* Practice positions attempted.
* Accuracy.
* Tactical success rate.
* Opening recognition.
* Most common mistakes.
* Performance against different Black setups.
* Positions requiring review.

Provide a simple dashboard showing:

**London System Progress**

with a few meaningful indicators rather than a large amount of statistics.

---

## 12. Windows Application

Build this as a modern Windows desktop application.

Initial priorities:

1. Fast and responsive.
2. Simple interface.
3. Chessboard-focused.
4. Easy to extend.
5. Local-first where practical.

The application should not require cloud services for its core functionality.

User progress should be stored locally.

The architecture should make it possible to add later:

* More chess openings.
* More lessons.
* More tactical exercises.
* Imported PGN games.
* Game analysis.
* Additional training modes.

The initial version, however, should remain tightly focused on the London System.

---

## 13. Suggested Initial Technology Direction

Use a technology stack that works well with Claude Code and is straightforward to maintain.

A sensible starting point would be:

* **C# / .NET**
* **WinUI 3** or another modern Windows UI framework.
* **Stockfish** as the chess engine.
* **SQLite** for local progress and training data.
* **FEN/PGN** support for chess positions and games.

Keep the chess rules, engine integration, lesson system and UI separated so they can evolve independently.

---

## 14. MVP

The first version should NOT attempt to build the entire product.

The MVP should contain:

### 1. Interactive chessboard

### 2. London System introductory lesson

Teach the basic setup and explain the purpose of each move.

### 3. "What would you play?" trainer

Start with approximately 20 carefully selected positions.

### 4. Basic Stockfish integration

Allow the application to evaluate the user's move and explain the difference.

### 5. Three Black setups

Initially support:

* ...d5 / ...Nf6
* ...Bf5
* ...g6

### 6. Progress tracking

Record results and identify weak areas.

### 7. Basic computer opponent

Allow the user to play a short game beginning from a London position.

The architecture should be designed so that additional openings, lessons and exercises can be added later.

---

## 15. Development Approach

Use Claude Code as an implementation partner.

Develop the application incrementally.

Each stage should produce a usable application rather than attempting to build the entire system at once.

Suggested sequence:

**Phase 1 — Foundation**

Create the Windows application, chessboard and basic navigation.

**Phase 2 — Chess**

Implement legal chess moves, positions, move history and PGN/FEN handling.

**Phase 3 — London Lessons**

Implement the first London System lessons.

**Phase 4 — Training**

Add interactive "What would you play?" exercises.

**Phase 5 — Stockfish**

Add engine evaluation and move explanations.

**Phase 6 — Adaptive Learning**

Track mistakes and generate targeted exercises.

**Phase 7 — Play**

Add computer games beginning from London positions.

**Phase 8 — Polish**

Improve the UX, visual design, explanations and progress dashboard.

---

## 16. Important Design Principle

The application must never become merely:

**"Here is the London System move tree. Memorise it."**

The desired outcome is:

> "I see the position, I understand what Black is trying to do, I know the plans available to me, and I can choose a good move without needing to remember a variation."

That is the measure of whether the application has succeeded.

## 17. First Development Task for Claude Code

Begin by producing:

1. A proposed technical architecture.
2. The recommended Windows technology stack and justification.
3. The project/folder structure.
4. The data model for lessons, positions, exercises and progress.
5. A minimal working chessboard application.
6. A first London System lesson.
7. A small set of training positions.
8. A roadmap showing the next development increments.

Do not build the entire application in one step. Establish a clean foundation that can be tested and extended incrementally.

I’d use this as the **master brief**, then give Claude Code smaller implementation prompts derived from it. That tends to work much better than asking Claude to build the whole application from one giant prompt.

The particularly important idea here is **adaptive teaching**: the app should learn *your* recurring London mistakes and keep feeding you positions that address them.
